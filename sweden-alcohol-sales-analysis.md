# Explaining Sweden’s alcohol sales, 1861–2006

The graph suggests that **alcohol restrictions shaped the sharp changes, while changing drinking culture shaped the longer trends**. Several historical forces contributed; the graph alone cannot establish their individual causal effects.

## What the graph measures

The chart shows recorded alcohol sales in **litres of pure alcohol per inhabitant aged 15 and older**, divided into spirits (blue), beer (yellow), wine (red), and total sales (green). These are alcohol-equivalent quantities, not litres of the beverages themselves.

The supplied chart cites *CAN Report 113, Drogutvecklingen i Sverige 2008*, Table 1, and covers 1861–2006.

## Historical explanations

| Period or pattern | Main explanation |
|---|---|
| **High spirits sales in the 1800s** | Sweden had a predominantly brännvin-based drinking culture. Economic conditions and harvests contributed to fluctuations. |
| **Decline towards the early 1900s** | Expanding local sales monopolies, beer taxes and other restrictions helped reduce sales. |
| **Sharp collapse around 1917–18** | First World War restrictions coincided with severe shortages. Despite its neutrality, Sweden experienced shortages of grain, potatoes and other basic foods. |
| **Low levels during the interwar years** | The motbok rationing system, nationwide from 1919 until 1955, restricted purchases. |
| **Rise in the 1950s–70s** | Rationing ended in 1955. Mellanöl became available in grocery stores in 1965, contributing to increased beer sales. Its removal from grocery stores in 1977 helps explain the subsequent decline. |

These explanations draw on [CAN’s historical analysis of Swedish alcohol sales](https://nordicwelfare.org/popnad/en/artiklar/swedens-drinking-habits-162-years-of-bottoms-up-and-crackdowns/) and [research on Sweden’s wartime society and food shortages](https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/wartime-and-post-war-societies-sweden/).

## Why wine replaced spirits

The later shift towards wine was partly deliberately encouraged. In 1957, Systembolaget launched **Operation Vin**, a campaign intended to steer consumers towards wine instead of spirits.

This provides a concrete policy contribution to the shift, although it cannot explain the entire decades-long change in preferences. The graph does not isolate the campaign’s effect from broader cultural changes. See [historical research on the state’s role in consumer culture](https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S1755750X24000025).

Because the chart already measures pure alcohol, rising wine sales represent a growing contribution to alcohol sales, not simply larger beverage volumes caused by wine’s lower alcohol concentration.

## Limits of the interpretation

**Recorded sales are not identical to total consumption.** Home distillation, smuggling and purchases abroad are not fully captured. Changes in those sources can affect the relationship between recorded sales and actual drinking. [CAN discusses this limitation in its historical series](https://nordicwelfare.org/popnad/en/artiklar/swedens-drinking-habits-162-years-of-bottoms-up-and-crackdowns/).

The graph also does not show drinking frequency, binge drinking, the distribution of consumption among people, or alcohol-related harm. It supports an account of changing sales and beverage preferences, but cannot independently establish how those outcomes changed.
